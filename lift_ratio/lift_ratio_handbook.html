1. Purpose and appropriate use

Larval source management (LSM) programmes need reliable surveillance, suitable interventions and a feasible allocation of field effort. When a programme cannot visit every candidate habitat immediately, a priority ranking can inform which habitats to assess first. The evaluation question is specific: what proportion of a measured entomological outcome is represented in the habitats selected at a given visit budget? This handbook describes cumulative outcome coverage and a full-range normalized coverage lift-ratio for that question. These measures complement, rather than replace, surveillance and intervention evaluation. [1]

Suitable inputs are a defined set of evaluation units, comparable observed outcomes and a priority score for every unit. A unit might be a habitat or a habitat visit; the outcome might be a standardized larval or pupal count. Specify species, life stage, sampling effort and period. The score can come from remote sensing, another model or a prespecified expert rule. For predictive claims, evaluate scores generated without using the corresponding test outcomes in model development or selection.

Coverage refers to the observed outcome represented, not necessarily total habitat productivity. Summing sampled counts does not establish adult emergence, and summing densities or rates does not necessarily represent total abundance. The denominator is the evaluated population, not all habitats in a city unless the sampling frame supports that interpretation.

Coverage lift-ratio is a cumulative-gains/Gini-type summary, not a new mathematical statistic. This handbook proposes a reproducible convention for entomological use, not a universally superior metric or an endorsed discipline-wide standard. Compare methods on the same evaluation population, inspect the full curves and report coverage at prespecified operational budgets. There is no universal “good lift-ratio” threshold. [2] [3]

2. Calculation and interpretation

For n evaluation units, let yi be the nonnegative observed outcome and si the priority score. Sort units in decreasing priority and retain genuine zero outcomes. At the first k units, cumulative coverage is:

C(k/n) = (y(1) + … + y(k)) / Σi=1…n yi

The horizontal coordinate is the share of units selected; the vertical coordinate is the share of total observed outcome represented. If two of four habitats contain 60 of 100 observed larvae, the point is (50%, 60%). Join cumulative points with straight lines, including (0, 0) and (1, 1).

Random ordering has expected coverage Crandom(x) = x. One random realization need not follow that diagonal. The ideal reference sorts the same units by observed outcome, highest first. It is a retrospective ceiling, not a prediction available before future observation.

Normalize the available area advantage

Integrate over the complete selected-habitat range, 0 to 1. With A the signed area between the ranking and random curves, and B the area between ideal and ranking curves:

L = A / (A + B)

Equivalently, if Amodel and Aideal are absolute areas under the curves:

L = (Amodel − 0.5) / (Aideal − 0.5)

Thus A = Amodel − 0.5 and B = Aideal − Amodel. Contributions below random are subtracted from A.

Report three distinct quantities

QuantityInterpretation
Full-range lift-ratio, LThe fraction of the available area advantage recovered. L = 0.800 means 80% of that advantage, not 80% of larvae eliminated or disease reduced.
Coverage, C(q)The outcome share at a stated habitat budget. C(0.50) = 0.60 means the first half of habitats contains 60% of the observed outcome.
Budget-specific multiplier, C(q)/qCoverage relative to random expectation at that budget. Here 0.60/0.50 = 1.20, or 1.2 times random expected coverage.

Changing the selected budget changes coverage, not full-range lift-ratio. Curves can cross: the highest full-range lift-ratio need not give the greatest coverage at a small operational budget.

Integration, ties and whole-habitat budgets

Use trapezoids: Acurve = Σj=1…n (xj − xj−1)(Cj + Cj−1)/2. Retain full numerical precision. Partial-range integration is a different measure.

For exact score ties, use the straight chord across each tied block: expected coverage under random ordering within that block. Do not break ties using outcomes or advantageous CSV row order. Rounding scores can create new ties.

The app interpolates at fraction q and separately reports k = floor(qn) whole habitats. For 743 units, 30% is 222.9 habitat equivalents, not a literal visit count. Expected tied-block coverage is not guaranteed for a particular selection.

Range, undefined cases and mathematical properties

For heterogeneous nonnegative outcomes, −1 ≤ L ≤ 1. Ideal ordering gives 1; reverse-outcome ordering gives −1; random expectation is 0. All-tied scores give the diagonal exactly. All-zero outcomes have undefined coverage and lift-ratio. Uniform positive outcomes have coverage C(x) = x but undefined lift-ratio. Report N/A, not a numerical zero; a very small ideal advantage also warrants numerical caution.

Strictly increasing score transformations preserve lift-ratio if ties remain unchanged. Positive rescaling of outcomes preserves coverage and lift-ratio. Adding a common nonnegative constant preserves defined normalized lift-ratio but changes coverage. Similar lift-ratio across cohorts therefore need not imply similar operational value. For binary outcomes with both classes present, equal unit weights and consistent ties, L = 2 × ROC AUC − 1; this identity does not extend indiscriminately to counts. [3]

3. A simple worked example

Suppose a priority rule orders four habitats with recorded larval counts of 40, 20, 30 and 10. There are 100 larvae in total. The scores determine the order; their numerical scale is irrelevant. These invented observations make the calculation transparent.

Trapezoidal integration gives a ranking area of 0.600 and an ideal area of 0.625. The random area is 0.500. Therefore, L = (0.600 − 0.500) / (0.625 − 0.500) = 0.800. This ranking achieves 80% of the full-curve gain that was possible over random ordering on these four observations.

At a budget of two habitats, the ranking covers 60% of the observed count. Its budget-specific multiplier is 60% / 50% = 1.20. Neither 0.800 nor 1.20 is the proportion of larvae eliminated, and neither measures monetary savings.

Compare the other measures on these same four habitats

To illustrate count-error metrics, we additionally assume hypothetical count predictions of [40, 20, 30, 10], equal to ten times the priority score. This count scale is an explicit teaching assumption, not something that can be inferred from a rank score.

Read the measures together. Count-error measures assess numerical predictions; rank correlations assess ordering agreement; NDCG assesses discounted gain; coverage and lift-ratio assess allocation of the measured outcome across habitat budgets. A larger numerical value under one metric is not evidence that it is a better metric. In particular, NDCG 0.982 and lift-ratio 0.800 use different denominators and different random-reference values. [5] [6] [4]

Binary ROC and precision-recall: define the positive class first

For larval presence (count > 0), every habitat is positive. ROC AUC is undefined because there are no negatives. Average precision is 1.000 for every ordering, so it provides no discrimination information here. Selecting any two habitats gives 100% precision and 50% recall of positive habitats, despite different possible larval-count coverage.

For a separate teaching question, define “high-count” as observed count ≥ 30. This arbitrary cutoff is not a recommended operational threshold. H1 and H2 are positive, and the priority-ordered labels become [1, 0, 1, 0].

Table 3. Binary evaluation after explicitly changing the target to count ≥ 30.
MeasureValueInterpretation

Average precision (AP)0.833Positive habitats occur at positions 1 and 3: AP = (1 + 2/3)/2. AP summarizes precision-recall using recall increments, not trapezoidal PR area.
Precision@2 and recall@250.0% and 50.0%One of the two selected habitats is high-count, and one of the two high-count habitats is selected. These are habitat-class fractions, not the 60% larval-count coverage.

Binary metrics are appropriate when identifying a specified class is the objective. They answer a changed question, not a defective version of the count-ranking question. [5] AP definition.

Same ranking, worse count predictions: a calibration counterexample

Double the hypothetical predictions to [80, 40, 60, 20]. Their ordering is unchanged. Lift-ratio remains 0.800, two-habitat coverage remains 60%, and Spearman, Kendall, NDCG and the binary discrimination measures above also stay unchanged. Count errors, however, increase:

Table 4. Rescaling predictions changes count accuracy without changing priority order.

The negative R² means these doubled predictions have more squared error than predicting the observed mean for every habitat. A useful ranking can still give poor absolute counts. Report count-error measures when numerical prediction matters, and coverage measures when prioritization matters. Neither replaces the other.

Formulas and reproducible metric conventions for this comparison

Let ei = predicted count − observed count. MAE = Σ|ei|/4; RMSE = √(Σei²/4); R² = 1 − Σei²/Σ(yi − 25)². Here squared error is 200 and the observed sum of squared deviations is 500.

Without ties, Spearman ρ = 1 − 6Σdi²/[n(n² − 1)], where d is the difference between observed and predicted ranks. Here Σd² = 2. Kendall τ-b = (concordant pairs − discordant pairs)/6 for this tie-free example.

Use linear count gain and discount 1/log2(r + 1): DCG@k = Σr=1…k y(r)/log2(r + 1), and NDCG@k = DCG@k / ideal DCG@k. For all four habitats, the areas are not involved: DCG = 71.925 and ideal DCG = 73.235. Exponential gain, another discount or another cutoff defines a different convention. NDCG implementation reference.

    EMPIRICAL CONTEXT AND CONTROLLED EXAMPLES

    4. Field cases and realistic simulations

    Field data help identify which features a teaching example should preserve and which conclusions it cannot support. The examples below sample synthetic counts from the observed count distribution in the Jigjiga study and assign artificial ranking scores. They do not reproduce the study's prediction results.

    4.1. Observed distributions from Jigjiga

    The complete labelled survey cohort contains 743 habitats. The 547-habitat cohort is its nested subset with segmentation-mask availability, not a second independent sample and not a positive-only cohort. Both distributions include habitats with zero recorded larvae. Their differences reflect the composition of these particular samples; they do not establish geographical or temporal transferability.

    The top-10% and top-30% shares use descending observed-count order and interpolate at the exact habitat fraction. They describe retrospective outcome concentration, not predictive performance. Counts are reported as recorded; this handbook does not establish standardized sampling effort, adult emergence, or treatment response.

    Source frequency counts and provenance

    Only count-frequency pairs and aggregate summaries are embedded here. Source-file hashes, cohort definitions and verification notes are available in the . No original habitat identifiers, coordinates, image features or prediction scores are included in these examples.

    4.2. From a source distribution to a controlled simulation

    For each new row, the generator samples a recorded count with replacement from the chosen cohort's frequency distribution. This preserves the empirical marginal distribution in expectation, including its zero prevalence and upper tail, but a particular sample will not necessarily have the original total, median or number of zero-count habitats. Sampling with replacement does not reproduce habitat identity, dependence between nearby habitats, repeated visits, sampling effort or persistence through time.

    The ranking scores are deliberately artificial. The informative scenario combines the logarithm of one plus the simulated count with specified random noise; other scenarios assign independent random scores, reverse the outcome ordering, or use perfect retrospective information. These mechanisms teach how ranking quality affects the curve. They are not fitted predictive models, recovered out-of-fold scores or simulations calibrated to the study's reported lift-ratio.

    Within one source distribution, keep the sample size and seed fixed while changing the score-quality scenario. Separate random-number streams preserve the same synthetic outcomes and identifiers, allowing a paired comparison of ordering alone. Changing the source distribution, sample size or seed changes the underlying example.

    The buttons select source distributions and example sizes, not actual study rows. All generated identifiers begin with SYN. A useful synthetic ranking does not demonstrate biological validity, transferable prediction, cost savings or malaria reduction.

    4.3. Field evidence and the choice of outcome

    Yared and colleagues reported that 23% of the positive habitats analysed in January accounted for 77% of the larvae in their Ethiopian study. This is a retrospective concentration result, with positive habitats as the denominator; it should not be interpreted as coverage of all screened premises or as evidence that a prediction model identified those habitats in advance. Such concentration motivates targeted prioritisation, while the changing contribution of habitats across months cautions against treating a productive habitat as permanently productive. [9]

    The Kenyan study by Orondo and colleagues distinguishes larval density from adult emergence when examining habitat productivity. That distinction is important for coverage analysis: recorded larval counts and emerging adult mosquitoes are different endpoints. A ranking that represents a large share of one measured outcome does not, by itself, establish the same coverage for another endpoint or demonstrate a reduction in malaria transmission. [10]

    5. Reporting and reusable resources

    5.1 Minimum reporting requirements

    On first mention, use full-range normalized coverage lift-ratio, give L = A/(A + B), and state the metric convention version. Report the full curve and coverage at prespecified budgets alongside lift-ratio.

    1. Population and unit: location, season, dates, inclusion criteria, row unit and number of distinct habitats.
    2. Outcome: species, life stage, sampling protocol, effort, units, total, median and zero fraction.
    3. Score and validation: algorithm or expert rule, version, direction, available inputs, data splits and model-selection procedure.
    4. Metric convention: full 0–1 range, trapezoids, exact-score ties, missing-value policy and undefined-result handling.
    5. Results and comparisons: lift-ratio, model and ideal areas, complete curves and coverage at stated fractional or whole-habitat budgets; use the same rows and outcomes for compared methods.
    6. Uncertainty and scope: resampling unit, interval method, dependence assumptions and operational outcomes not measured.
    7. Reproducibility: data-access conditions, code/metric version, simulation seeds where applicable and sufficient exported values to reconstruct the curve.
    Suggested reporting sentence

    “Among [n] [evaluation units] with [outcome definition], [priority method] achieved full-range normalized coverage lift-ratio [L] under [validation design]. The first [k of n units or exact fraction q] represented [C]% of the observed outcome, compared with [q]% under random-selection expectation. We used full-range trapezoidal integration and expected ordering within exact score ties. [Uncertainty method and interval, if justified].”

    The generated report records chosen settings. It does not verify sampling comparability, declared metadata or the independence of uploaded predictions.

    5.2 Validation and operational boundaries

    Held-out evaluation, model selection and dependence

    Held-out outcomes must be excluded from model development and selection. In out-of-fold (OOF) prediction, each row is predicted by a model fitted without its test fold. Imputation, scaling, supervised feature selection and tuning must respect the evaluation design. Repeated selection using the reported test results can still cause optimism; use nested selection or an untouched final evaluation set where appropriate. [7]

    Group repeated visits by habitat and use spatial or temporal holdouts when they match intended deployment. Such tests answer different generalization questions; they are not interchangeable. [8] This app pools supplied rows into one curve. Pooled OOF ranking requires scores comparable across folds, and its lift-ratio is not the mean of fold-specific lifts.

    Uncertainty and comparison between rankings

    The tools provide descriptive point estimates, not confidence intervals. For fixed test-set scores, resampling must respect the sampling unit and spatial, temporal or repeated-measure dependence. Compare rankings through paired resampling of their shared outcomes and scores. Recompute both areas and normalization in every replicate and report undefined replicates. Fixed-score resampling excludes model-training uncertainty. Repeated cross-validation partitions reuse observations; their standard deviation is not an independent-sample confidence interval.

    From habitat selection to programme outcomes

    The horizontal axis weights each unit equally. It is not a cost axis: travel, access, service duration and team constraints require separate assessment. High lift-ratio or earlier scheduled coverage does not demonstrate net savings, reduced adult emergence or reduced disease. Missing habitats, measurement error and changing conditions limit the evaluated population. Low priority does not justify permanently excluding a habitat from surveillance. Verify locally and evaluate treatment and transferability prospectively. [1]
